# Marble Track 3 — the physical thing

## Status
In progress — interviews with Rob, 2026-08-14 onward. Quotes are his exact words.

---

## The rotating stage

**The first version of MT3 did not rotate.** That is why there is a second version.

> "Because there was no rotation of stage, I quickly found that I could not easily
> reach the back of the project, which meant that I would have to move the entire
> thing or work in a very uncomfortable position. So to make the final iteration, I
> knew fundamentally that the stage has to rotate."

So the single most distinctive thing about MT3's rig — a marble track that turns —
exists because Rob's arms couldn't reach the back.

### The bearing

Bought at a home centre. It is a **Chinese restaurant table turntable bearing** (a
lazy Susan), and it is mismatched to the job in an instructive way:

> "A restaurant table is quite heavy so it has good momentum and rolls nicely. But
> my stage is so light that it doesn't have any momentum, and the bearing sometimes
> catches and makes it hard to rotate a small amount to a specific location."

The bearing was designed for weight. The stage is popsicle sticks. Fine positioning
— which is the entire job in stop-motion — is exactly what it's worst at.

### What rotation gives, and what it costs

**Gives:** access to parts that would otherwise be facing the blue background.

**Costs:**

> "When characters are suspended from threads tied to the fixed structure of my desk,
> that makes the rotation difficult or impossible to move without twisting the
> threads all around. So it doesn't always work as magically easy as I hoped it
> would."

The stage rotates; the desk does not. Any Worker hanging on a thread — G Choppy
flying, anyone suspended — pins the stage in place until they're down. Two systems
that each solve a problem, quietly fighting each other.

## The first version is gone

The original non-rotating MT3 — the one **Pinky** worked on, dated to 2011 in
`docs/worker_descriptions_archive_2026_04_07.md` — no longer exists:

> "The original track that Pinky worked on does not exist anywhere that I'm aware
> of."

Not dismantled and stored, not photographed into an archive. Gone. Pinky is
therefore the only Worker who has been through both attempts, and the only surviving
witness to a track nobody else has seen. Squarehead's "day one" is 2017; hers is six
years earlier.

### The spare slab

One physical object does survive from that era, and it's the best single artefact in
the whole story:

> "I do have a piece of wood that I bought at the same time, thinking that I would
> make a set of tracks — but I had no idea it would take a million years to finish
> even one."

He bought lumber for a **series**. One slab became MT3. The other is still a plain
piece of wood, holding the place of a track that hasn't been started. It is the
ambition and the reality sitting next to each other in the same room.

*(Open: is that slab earmarked for MT4?)*

### The unopened box

The same shopping trip produced the project's best statistic:

> "At the same time I was buying materials, I bought 2 boxes of 1,000 craft sticks
> and one of the boxes hasn't even been opened. From the original box I did open
> I've only used at most maybe 5% of the sticks."

Two thousand craft sticks bought. **At most about fifty used, in nine years.** One
box has never been opened at all. (Product:
`https://www.robnugen.com/en/sayonara/items/creatology-wood-craft-sticks/`)

Together with the spare slab, this is the same joke told twice in physical objects:
Rob equipped himself for a series of marble tracks and has not yet finished one.
MT3 is 95% of one box of sticks.

## The rig

Three desks' worth of equipment, all pointing the same way:

- **Lemur 2** (laptop) sits on the left side of Rob's desk, **rotated 90°** so its
  camera points off the left end of the desk.
- A **monitor above it** swivels about 45° in the same direction, so Rob can sit or
  stand at the next desk over — the marble track's desk — and still see it.
- A **Canon Kiss** camera points the same direction, focused only on the track, with
  its focal point **50 cm at the centre of the track**. Rob's photographer friend
  **Mark Wild** taught him how to get a deep enough depth of field that the whole
  track stays in focus.
- The **blue background is fabric**, and it is **glued** to the desk and the stage —
  physically attached so it cannot shift between frames.

That last detail is the whole discipline in miniature: in a medium where the frames
never lie, a backdrop that drifts a millimetre is a ruined afternoon, so the backdrop
gets glued down.

## The cost of restarting

As of 2026-08-14 the track is **covered in plastic and has not been touched for at
least nine months.** Getting back to it costs *at least an hour* before a single
photo:

> * Uncover the track
> * Find a place to fold the cover
> * Remember how to run Dragonframe and the live stream software
> * Realign all of the characters on the track — "places, everyone!"
> * Figure out what each character is doing
> * Figure out what is the next position for each character
> * Move each character to the next position
> * Check and double check and pray I remembered everything
> * Take the first photo in 9 months

After that hour of friction: *"I could probably take two or even 3 more photos within
the next 60 minutes."*

So a returning session is roughly **one hour of setup for one third of a second of
movie.**

## Between sessions

The rule is continuity-driven, not tidiness-driven:

> "If I think I'll be back the next day or two, I leave everything exactly as is so
> it's easier to go to the next frame next time. Only once it has become a month+ of
> no filming, I put the cover back on, which knocks the characters around but keeps
> them less dusty."

The cover is a trade: dust protection at the cost of scrambling the scene. Which is
precisely why the return costs an hour of "places, everyone!" — **a long pause
physically disturbs the set.** The gap in production isn't just lost time; it
destroys the arrangement that made resuming cheap.

## The livestream

- Rob is **mostly explaining to the audience but not looking at the camera** — his
  attention is on the characters.
- **No music.** It would distract him, and he has no mixer to make it sound good for
  viewers.
- When he has to stop — glue drying, or brain melting — he just explains the
  situation on stream.
- Sessions can be very short: *"In some cases I get only a 15 minute live stream
  before it's like, 'Oops, too bad I have to wait for the glue to dry which will take
  2 hours.'"*
- He has never tried leaving the stream running for the two hours while glue dries.

## The pattern: every distinctive feature is a workaround

This keeps recurring, and it may be the truest single thing about MT3. Nothing that
makes it charming was chosen for charm. Each signature feature is a solution to a
physical constraint:

| Signature feature | The problem it solves |
|---|---|
| **Small scale** (popsicle sticks, toothpicks) | Rob sees well very close up; bowling-ball-scale tracks don't suit his eyes |
| **Workers as characters** | A months-long time-lapse of a man gluing sticks would look janky |
| **Rotating stage** | Couldn't reach the back of the project |
| **Double-shot composites** | Pipe-cleaner figures won't balance where they're needed |
| **Softwood + slow-drying glue** | LEGO is too opaque and its roof tiles too sloped for a satisfying run |

Worth saying out loud in public: the whimsy is engineering. Every gag has a
constraint underneath it.
